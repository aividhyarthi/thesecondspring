Weight-loss medicines that act on incretin pathways have changed the treatment landscape for obesity. But weight reduction can involve changes in both fat mass and lean mass. This has led to a question in men's health: could testosterone treatment help preserve muscle when men use these medicines?

A systematic review published online on 19 November 2026 examined the available evidence. Its conclusion is cautious: current research does not establish that adding testosterone to incretin-based treatment provides an additional benefit for body composition or muscle outcomes. The review also found major gaps in evidence on strength, physical function and safety.

This matters because testosterone is sometimes promoted as a way to protect muscle or improve weight-loss results. The new review does not support using testosterone for that purpose without a proper medical indication.

## What question did the review ask?

The paper, *Testosterone Added to Incretin Therapy in Men With Overweight or Obesity: A Systematic Review of Body Composition Evidence and Gaps*, examined studies comparing testosterone replacement therapy alongside an incretin regimen with a comparable regimen without testosterone, or studies that examined baseline androgen status over time.

The researchers searched seven databases and two registries through 4 September 2026. They assessed study quality using established tools and graded the certainty of the evidence.

Only two reports provided body-composition outcomes that could be considered, and the measures were not equivalent. The authors judged the evidence to be very low certainty.

## What did the review find?

One non-randomised pilot report included only five men in each group. The reported final difference in lean mass was imprecise and compatible with no clear benefit.

A separate randomised conference report involved 266 men across five treatment arms. It reported different proportions of weight loss attributed to fat-free mass in groups with and without testosterone. However, the comparison-arm sizes were not available in the review's account, and the measures could not be treated as equivalent to the pilot study.

The review concluded that neither report established an additional benefit from testosterone. It also found no usable testosterone-specific comparisons for strength, physical function or safety, and no eligible baseline-androgen analyses that could resolve the question.

The conclusion is not that testosterone never has a role in men's health. It is that the evidence does not support adding it to incretin treatment solely to preserve lean tissue.

## Why is muscle loss an important question during weight reduction?

Body weight is made up of different components, including fat, muscle and other lean tissues. A reduction on the weighing scale does not reveal exactly what has changed.

Muscle contributes to strength, balance, mobility and everyday function. Preserving muscle becomes especially important with age, when people may be more vulnerable to frailty and loss of independence.

For this reason, weight-management care should look beyond kilograms lost. Clinicians may consider nutrition, resistance exercise, physical function, body composition where appropriate, and the person's medical history.

The review highlights an evidence gap: researchers need better studies that measure not only lean mass, but also strength, function and treatment safety.

## Does low testosterone explain weight gain in every man after 40?

No. Tiredness, reduced motivation, weight gain, lower libido and changes in muscle mass can have many possible causes. They do not establish testosterone deficiency on their own.

A diagnosis of hypogonadism requires compatible symptoms or signs together with consistently low testosterone levels, assessed appropriately. Clinicians may repeat early-morning measurements and investigate possible causes. Obesity, sleep disorders, diabetes, medicines and other health conditions can complicate interpretation.

A man should not use testosterone based on age, an online symptom checklist or a single laboratory result. Treatment decisions require an individual assessment and a discussion of potential benefits, risks and monitoring.

## What about men using GLP-1 or other incretin-based medicines?

The review does not recommend combining testosterone with these medicines. It says the available evidence is insufficient to establish an additional muscle or body-composition benefit.

Men using prescribed weight-management medicines should discuss nutrition, physical activity and any concerns about muscle loss with their treating clinician. They should not add testosterone or stop a prescribed medicine based on headlines or supplement marketing.

## What research is needed next?

The review identifies several gaps. Larger, well-designed studies need to compare similar treatment groups and report clearly defined body-composition outcomes. They should also assess strength, physical function, adverse events and longer-term outcomes.

The field needs to distinguish men with clinically confirmed testosterone deficiency from men whose testosterone levels are not low. Without that distinction, it is difficult to know who might benefit from treatment and who may be exposed to unnecessary risk.

## The takeaway

The new systematic review does not establish that testosterone protects muscle when added to incretin-based weight-loss treatment in men with overweight or obesity. The evidence is very limited, and important outcomes such as strength, function and safety remain unanswered.

For men after 40, the sensible approach is to assess the reason for symptoms, manage metabolic health and preserve muscle through an individualised clinical plan. Testosterone is a medical treatment for specific indications, not a general anti-ageing or weight-loss add-on.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does testosterone help men lose more weight with GLP-1 medicines?**

The review did not establish that adding testosterone provides an additional weight-loss or body-composition benefit.

**Can testosterone prevent muscle loss during weight reduction?**

This review found very low-certainty evidence and did not establish that testosterone prevents lean-mass loss in men using incretin treatment.

**Should every man over 40 test testosterone?**

Age alone does not mean every man needs testing. Testing is generally considered when symptoms or clinical signs suggest deficiency, with results interpreted by a qualified clinician.

**Is tiredness a sign of low testosterone?**

Tiredness has many possible causes. It is not enough on its own to diagnose testosterone deficiency.

**Is testosterone a general anti-ageing treatment?**

No. Testosterone should be used only when there is a recognised clinical indication and after an appropriate assessment. This review does not support using it solely to preserve lean mass during weight-loss treatment.
