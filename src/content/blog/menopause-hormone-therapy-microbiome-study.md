Why do some women experience meaningful relief from menopause symptoms with treatment while others have a different response? A study published in *Nature Medicine* on 3 November 2026 explores one possible part of that question: the communities of bacteria living in different parts of the body.

The research followed 356 perimenopausal women receiving menopausal hormone therapy for six months. It found changes in vaginal and urinary microbial communities during treatment, and reported that the microbial patterns present before treatment were associated with differences in some outcomes.

The findings offer an emerging line of research into the relationship between hormones and the microbiome. They do not mean that women should seek commercial microbiome tests before discussing treatment.

## What did the study do?

Researchers enrolled women aged 40 to 55 who were experiencing menopausal symptoms at 14 centres in China. Participants received a standard oral regimen of estradiol and dydrogesterone as part of their care and were assessed over 24 weeks.

The team analysed samples from several sites, including the tongue, stool, vaginal and cervical area, and urine. They used metagenomic sequencing to examine microbial communities and compared those patterns with symptom and health measures over time.

The researchers reported the most pronounced microbial restructuring in the vaginocervical and urinary sites. They observed an increase in *Lactobacillus crispatus* and a decrease in *Gardnerella vaginalis* in the vaginocervical samples after treatment. Changes in the gut were more limited.

## Why are these bacteria being studied?

The vaginal microbiome is the community of microorganisms that live in and around the vagina. Certain Lactobacillus species are commonly associated with a vaginal environment that is less favourable to the overgrowth of some potentially harmful bacteria.

Oestrogen influences the vaginal tissues and the environment in which these microorganisms live. Hormonal changes during perimenopause and menopause can therefore coincide with changes in the vaginal microbiome.

The new study adds detail by looking at several body sites and examining how microbial patterns changed during hormone therapy. It also found that baseline microbial community types were associated with different responses. For example, women with certain starting profiles showed more pronounced microbial changes than women with other profiles.

These associations are scientifically interesting. They do not prove that the bacteria caused symptom improvement, or that changing the microbiome would necessarily improve treatment outcomes.

## Does this mean a microbiome test can choose menopause treatment?

Not at present.

The researchers suggest that microbiome information may eventually contribute to more personalised menopause care. But this is an emerging research area. The study involved a defined group of women in China, a specific hormone regimen and a six-month observation period. It does not establish a validated test that clinicians can use routinely to select hormone therapy.

Women should not delay an appropriate medical consultation or start, stop or change hormone therapy based on a microbiome report. Treatment decisions need to consider symptoms, medical history, contraindications, preferences and the clinician's assessment. ## Why the research matters to Indian women

Vaginal and urinary symptoms are often difficult for women to discuss. Dryness, discomfort during sex, recurrent urinary symptoms and changes in sexual wellbeing can affect quality of life, yet some women may not mention them during a short appointment.

This study is a reminder that menopause-related health is not limited to periods and hot flashes. It also shows why more research is needed across different populations. The study was conducted in China, and its results should not automatically be generalised to Indian women, whose health experiences and microbial environments may differ.

Women experiencing vaginal or urinary symptoms can raise them with a gynaecologist. Similar symptoms can have several causes, including infections and other conditions, so they should not automatically be attributed to menopause.

## What comes next?

Researchers will need to replicate the findings in larger and more diverse populations, test whether the observed microbial patterns predict outcomes reliably, and establish whether microbiome-guided decisions improve care compared with standard clinical assessment.

The key question is not whether the microbiome is suddenly a treatment in itself. It is whether understanding the relationship between hormones, microbes and symptoms can eventually help clinicians make better-informed decisions.

## The bottom line

A November 2026 Nature Medicine study found that menopausal hormone therapy was accompanied by changes in vaginal and urinary microbial communities in a group of 356 perimenopausal women. Starting microbiome patterns were associated with differences in outcomes. The findings are promising, but microbiome-guided menopause treatment remains a research possibility rather than an established clinical service.
