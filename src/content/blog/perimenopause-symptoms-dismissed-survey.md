A woman in her late thirties or forties notices that her sleep has changed, her periods are less predictable and her mood feels unfamiliar. She wonders whether perimenopause could explain what is happening. But when she raises the possibility, she may hear that she is too young, too stressed or simply getting older.

A survey highlighted by the American College of Osteopathic Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOOG) on 2 November 2026 puts numbers to this experience. Among 1,000 US women aged 35 to 59 who were navigating perimenopause, 72% said they had faced pushback when they raised their symptoms as potentially related to perimenopause.

The finding is a reminder that recognising the menopause transition can be difficult, even when women are actively looking for answers.

## What did the survey find?

The survey was conducted in July 2026 by ThisIsMenopause, an online community for people navigating perimenopause and menopause. The ACOOG report highlighted several findings:

- 82% initially attributed their symptoms to anxiety, burnout or everyday stress.
- 72% reported pushback from clinicians, friends or family when they suggested perimenopause.
- 47% of respondents aged 35 to 39 said they had been told they were too young.
- 76% said they had to raise the possibility of perimenopause with a healthcare provider themselves.

Among respondents who discussed their symptoms with a provider, only 55% found the conversation helpful, according to the ACOOG summary.

The survey offers a useful snapshot of women's experiences. However, it was an online community survey of US respondents, not a representative survey of all women. Its percentages should not be presented as estimates for India or for the global population.

## Why can perimenopause be difficult to recognise?

Perimenopause does not begin in the same way for everyone. Changes in menstrual cycles may be an early sign, but some women first notice sleep problems, mood changes, fatigue or difficulty concentrating. Symptoms can overlap with stress, thyroid conditions, anaemia, sleep disorders, medication effects and other health concerns.

Age can also create confusion. Perimenopause often begins during the forties, but the timing varies. A woman should not be dismissed solely because she is younger than the average age of menopause.

At the same time, not every symptom in midlife is caused by changing hormones. A careful medical history and assessment are important because other conditions may need treatment.

## Why does being heard matter?

When women feel that their concerns are dismissed, they may delay seeking help or stop raising the issue. That can leave symptoms affecting sleep, relationships, work and everyday confidence without a clear plan for assessment.

A good consultation does not require a clinician to agree immediately that every symptom is due to perimenopause. It requires listening, asking about the pattern of symptoms, considering other possible causes and explaining what should happen next.

## What can women do before an appointment?

A short symptom record can make the conversation more useful. Women can note:

- Changes in menstrual dates, flow or cycle length.
- Sleep disruption, night sweats or hot flashes.
- Mood changes, anxiety or concentration difficulties.
- Vaginal, urinary or sexual symptoms.
- When symptoms began and how they affect daily activities.
- Medicines, supplements and relevant medical history.

A woman can ask whether perimenopause is a possible explanation, what other causes should be considered and whether any tests or follow-up are appropriate. Our guides to the [first signs of perimenopause](/blog/first-signs-of-perimenopause) and [how perimenopause is tested for](/blog/how-to-test-for-perimenopause) can help you prepare. A single hormone blood test may not provide a complete answer in the menopause transition, so the assessment should be guided by clinical context.

## The India question

The survey was conducted in the United States. India needs its own research to understand how women experience perimenopause, how often symptoms are recognised, what happens during consultations and whether access to menopause-informed care differs by geography, income or type of healthcare provider.

There is also a need for better public information in Indian languages. Women should be able to recognise common symptoms without assuming that every change is hormonal or that they must manage it alone.

## The bottom line

The survey suggests that many women in the United States struggle to have perimenopause taken seriously when they raise it. It is not proof that the same percentages apply in India, but it raises an important question for Indian healthcare: are women being given enough information and time to discuss the changes they are experiencing?
