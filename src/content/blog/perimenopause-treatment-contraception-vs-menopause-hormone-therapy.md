A woman in her 40s may still be having periods, but the pattern may have changed. One month the cycle arrives early. Another month it is late. Hot flashes, sleep disruption, mood changes or heavy bleeding may begin to affect daily life. At the same time, pregnancy may still be possible.

This creates a treatment conversation that is more complicated than simply asking whether a woman needs hormones.

A clinical toolkit published in *Obstetrics & Gynecology* in November 2026 looks at hormonal options for perimenopause. Its central point is practical: clinicians need to understand whether the main goal is contraception, menstrual cycle control, symptom relief, or a combination of these.

The options may overlap in some benefits, but they are not interchangeable. For background on two specific comparisons, see our existing articles on [birth control versus HRT during perimenopause](/blog/birth-control-vs-hrt-perimenopause) and [contraception options in perimenopause](/blog/contraception-in-perimenopause); this article focuses on the broader framework for matching treatment to goals.

## Why perimenopause needs a different conversation

Perimenopause is the transition leading up to menopause. Hormone levels can fluctuate rather than decline in a straight line. Periods may become irregular, and symptoms can include hot flashes, sleep problems, mood changes, cognitive complaints, sexual concerns and abnormal uterine bleeding.

A woman may want to avoid pregnancy, manage difficult bleeding and reduce hot flashes at the same time. Another may no longer need contraception but may want help with symptoms. A third may have a medical history that changes which treatments can be considered.

The toolkit argues for matching the approach to the woman's symptoms, reproductive goals and health risks rather than assuming that one hormonal option suits everyone.

## Contraceptive hormones and menopause hormone therapy do different jobs

This is one of the most important distinctions.

**Hormonal contraception** is designed to prevent pregnancy. Depending on the method, it may also regulate bleeding, reduce menstrual pain or influence some perimenopause symptoms. Some methods suppress ovulation.

**Menopause hormone therapy**, sometimes called menopausal hormone therapy or MHT, is designed to treat symptoms related to the menopause transition, particularly vasomotor symptoms such as hot flashes and night sweats. Standard systemic MHT should not be relied on to prevent pregnancy.

A woman who still needs contraception should not assume that taking MHT means pregnancy is no longer possible. She should ask her clinician how contraception will be managed alongside symptom treatment. If periods are continuing, our article on [starting HRT while still getting periods](/blog/starting-hrt-while-still-getting-periods) covers that specific situation in more depth.

## What does the new clinician toolkit add?

The article by Jaclyn Piasta and Amani Zewail describes hormonal contraceptives as one option for perimenopause care, while also discussing alternative hormonal strategies and individualised treatment.

It highlights that hormonal contraception may not be adequate or preferred for every woman. Some women may need a different approach because of their symptoms, bleeding pattern, contraceptive needs or health risks.

The authors present a clinical perspective and practical toolkit. This is not a randomised trial proving that one treatment is better than another, and it is not a universal prescribing guideline for every country.

## Questions worth discussing with a gynaecologist

A consultation can be more useful when the woman is clear about what she wants help with. These questions may help structure the conversation:

1. **Do I still need contraception?** Age and irregular periods alone do not establish that pregnancy is impossible.
2. **Which problem are we trying to address first?** Cycle control, heavy bleeding, hot flashes, sleep disruption and vaginal or urinary symptoms may need different assessments.
3. **Could another condition be causing my symptoms or bleeding?** Do not automatically attribute new or heavy bleeding to perimenopause.
4. **Would a contraceptive method or menopause hormone therapy address my main goals?** Ask what each option can and cannot do.
5. **Does my medical history affect the choices?** Share information about blood pressure, migraine, smoking, blood clots, cardiovascular disease, liver conditions, cancer history and current medicines.
6. **How will we review the plan?** Ask what improvement to expect, what side effects to watch for and when to return.

These are conversation prompts, not a self-selection checklist. A clinician needs to assess individual suitability.

## Why this matters for Indian women

In India, some women may seek help primarily for irregular periods or heavy bleeding, while hot flashes, sleep problems or mood changes remain unmentioned. Others may assume that once periods become irregular, pregnancy is no longer possible.

A consultation should make room for both reproductive and menopause-related concerns. It should also consider local clinical guidance, medicine availability, cost and the woman's medical history.

The 2026 toolkit was published in a US journal and is a clinical perspective. Its suggestions should not be presented as Indian national guidance. Indian clinicians should interpret the ideas alongside the Indian Menopause Society's 2026 clinical practice guidance and other relevant local recommendations.

## One more point: irregular bleeding needs attention

Perimenopause can change menstrual patterns, but new, persistent, unusually heavy or otherwise concerning bleeding should not simply be dismissed as hormones. A clinician may need to assess the pattern and consider other causes before treatment is chosen.

If bleeding is very heavy, causes faintness or severe weakness, or is accompanied by severe pain, seek prompt medical care.

## The takeaway

Perimenopause is not simply a milder version of menopause. Women may still need contraception while experiencing symptoms that deserve treatment. Hormonal contraception and menopause hormone therapy have different purposes, even when some effects overlap.

The useful question is not just "Which hormone should I take?" It is "What do I need help with, do I still need contraception, and which options are suitable for my health history?"

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I become pregnant during perimenopause?

Yes. Fertility declines with age, but pregnancy can still occur until menopause is established. If pregnancy is not wanted, discuss contraception with a qualified clinician.

### Does menopause hormone therapy prevent pregnancy?

Standard systemic menopause hormone therapy is not a contraceptive method. Ask a clinician about contraception if pregnancy remains possible.

### Is hormonal contraception the same as HRT?

No. Hormonal contraception is designed to prevent pregnancy and may help with cycle control. Menopause hormone therapy is used to treat menopause-related symptoms and does not reliably prevent ovulation.

### Does this toolkit tell every woman which treatment to use?

No. It is a clinical perspective that describes options and supports individualised decisions. It does not replace an individual medical assessment.
