A weighing scale gives one number. It does not tell you how much of that weight is muscle, how much is fat, or where the fat is stored. That distinction may become especially important around menopause, when many women notice changes in their waistline, strength and body shape even if their eating habits have not changed much.

A study published online on 17 November 2026 in the journal *Menopause* examined body composition and health outcomes in 3,439 women from the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis. Its findings add to the growing evidence that the menopause transition deserves attention as a period of changing metabolic and cardiovascular health.

## What did the study find?

The researchers examined estimated fat mass and fat-free mass across reproductive stages. In the overall group, each additional kilogram of estimated fat mass was associated with a 3% higher rate of major adverse cardiovascular events and a 3% higher rate of all-cause mortality. Each additional kilogram of fat-free mass was associated with a 4% lower rate of mortality.

The associations were stronger in the group classified as being in the menopause phase. In that group, each kilogram of estimated fat mass was associated with an 18% higher rate of mortality, while each kilogram of fat-free mass was associated with a 29% lower rate.

These are relative associations, not a prediction of an individual woman's risk. The confidence intervals for some menopause-phase estimates were wide, so the numbers should not be treated as precise personal risk calculations.

## Why muscle matters during midlife

Fat-free mass includes muscle, bone, organs and body water. It is not the same thing as muscle mass, although muscle makes up an important part of it.

Muscle supports everyday movement, balance, glucose use and physical independence. Losing muscle while gaining fat can change metabolic health even when total body weight changes very little. This is one reason waist measurement, strength and fitness can add useful context to the number on the scale.

## What the study cannot tell us

This was an observational analysis. It cannot prove that gaining fat caused the higher risk, or that building muscle would prevent deaths. Body composition was estimated using equations derived from bioimpedance measurements taken at a later study visit, rather than being directly measured at the original baseline. The authors also noted limitations in the size and precision of some subgroup estimates.

The research did not test a resistance-training programme, a diet, hormone therapy or any other intervention. It therefore cannot tell us which treatment changes outcomes.

## What can women discuss with their doctors?

A useful midlife health conversation can go beyond weight alone. Depending on personal history and symptoms, a clinician may consider blood pressure, cholesterol, blood glucose, waist measurement, physical activity, strength, bone health and other individual risk factors.

Strength-building activity, adequate nutrition and regular movement are sensible parts of healthy ageing for many adults. The right plan depends on health conditions, injuries, fitness and medical advice. Women should not start supplements, hormone therapy or restrictive diets based on a single observational study.

## The Indian context

In India, midlife women may be managing work, caregiving and household responsibilities while having little time for structured exercise. Strength activity does not necessarily require a gym, but it should be appropriate to the person's health and ability. A doctor or qualified physiotherapist can help someone who has joint pain, osteoporosis, heart disease or other medical conditions choose a safe starting point.

The practical message is not that women should chase a particular body weight. It is that preserving strength and understanding body composition may be important parts of health through the menopause transition.

## Frequently asked questions

**Does menopause automatically cause weight gain?**

No. Ageing, sleep, activity, diet, medicines, stress and hormonal changes can all influence body weight and composition. Individual experiences vary.

**Is fat-free mass the same as muscle?**

No. Fat-free mass includes muscle but also bone, organs and body water.

**Does this study prove that building muscle prevents heart disease?**

No. It found associations in an observational cohort. It did not test an exercise intervention or prove cause and effect.

**Should every woman get a body composition scan?**

The study does not establish that everyone needs a scan. Ask a clinician which measurements are useful given your health history and risk factors.
