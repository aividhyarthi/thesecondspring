A woman asking about menopause treatment may receive one recommendation from one doctor and a different explanation from another. That does not automatically mean one doctor is wrong. Treatment decisions can depend on the woman's symptoms, age, medical history, preferences and the evidence available for each option.

A systematic review published online on 24 November 2026 examined the quality and consistency of US clinical guidelines for menopause symptom treatment. Its findings raise a useful question for patients and clinicians: how clear is the evidence behind the advice women receive?

## What did the review examine?

The researchers searched PubMed and EMBASE for US menopause treatment guidelines and consensus statements published between 2014 and 2024. They screened 655 records and included 13 guidelines in their review.

The guidelines covered topics such as hormone therapy for hot flashes, genitourinary symptoms, premature ovarian insufficiency, sleep disturbance, depression and hypoactive sexual desire disorder. They also considered non-hormonal options.

The review found that seven guidelines were of moderate quality, four were rated low to moderate, and two were rated low. None received a high-quality rating. The authors identified gaps in areas such as stakeholder involvement and how easily recommendations could be applied in practice.

The review also found that guidelines did not provide decision-support tools or clear treatment-sequencing algorithms for choosing among non-hormonal therapies.

## Does that mean menopause guidelines cannot be trusted?

No. The findings need to be read carefully.

The review did not conclude that every recommendation was wrong or that all menopause treatments lack evidence. It assessed the quality of the guideline documents and the strength and consistency of the evidence supporting them.

The review noted that some non-hormonal treatments, including SSRIs, SNRIs, gabapentin and fezolinetant, were supported by moderate- to high-quality evidence in the guidelines it examined. It also found that many guidelines advised against over-the-counter or alternative treatments because the supporting data were limited.

A guideline can contain useful recommendations while still having weaknesses in how it was developed, how clearly it explains the evidence or how well it helps a clinician choose between options.

## Why can recommendations differ?

There are several reasons.

**Different questions are being answered.** One guideline may focus on hot flashes, another on vaginal and urinary symptoms, and another on mood or sleep.

**The evidence changes.** New medicines, safety information and clinical studies can lead professional organisations to update recommendations at different times.

**Women do not all have the same health history.** A treatment that may be suitable for one woman may be unsuitable for another because of a history of thrombosis, cardiovascular disease, liver disease, cancer or other factors.

**Guidelines may use different methods.** Organisations may assess evidence, define outcomes and grade recommendation strength differently.

**Access matters.** A treatment may be supported by evidence but unavailable, unaffordable or difficult to obtain in a particular health system.

## What does this mean for women in India?

The review examined US guidelines, not Indian clinical practice. Its results should not be presented as an audit of Indian doctors or Indian hospitals.

India has its own 2026 Clinical Practice Guidelines for Menopause from the Indian Menopause Society. That is an important local reference, and women should discuss treatment options in the context of Indian guidance, locally available medicines, affordability and their own medical history.

The wider lesson is that a recommendation should come with an explanation. Women deserve to know what evidence supports an option, what uncertainty remains and why a clinician believes it may or may not suit them.

## Five questions that can make a consultation clearer

1. Which symptom are we trying to treat first?
2. What evidence supports this treatment for someone with my health history?
3. What are the alternatives, including non-hormonal options?
4. What side effects, interactions or warning signs should I know about?
5. When will we review whether the treatment is helping?

These questions do not replace clinical advice. They help make shared decision-making more practical.

## What should better guidelines look like?

The authors argue for clearer decision frameworks that connect treatment options with contraindications, patient preferences and risk profiles. Such tools could help clinicians explain why one option is being considered and another is not.

Guidelines should also be updated as evidence changes, include the perspectives of patients and clinicians, and make the limits of the evidence easy to understand.

## The bottom line

The November 2026 review found variation in the quality of US menopause treatment guidelines and a lack of structured tools for selecting non-hormonal treatments. It does not mean that all advice is unreliable. It shows why transparent evidence, local clinical guidance and shared decisions matter.

Women should not feel they have to choose between blindly accepting advice and trying to interpret medical studies alone. A good clinician should help explain the evidence in the context of the individual woman.

## Frequently asked questions

### Did the review study Indian menopause guidelines?

No. It examined US guidelines and consensus statements published from 2014 to 2024.

### Did the review find that hormone therapy does not work?

No. It assessed guideline quality and the evidence behind recommendations. It did not conduct a new treatment trial.

### Why might two doctors recommend different treatments?

They may be considering different symptoms, medical histories, treatment risks, patient preferences or available options. Ask each clinician to explain the reasoning.
