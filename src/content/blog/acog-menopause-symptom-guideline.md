A new clinical practice guideline from the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) puts the management of menopause-related symptoms back in the spotlight. Published in the November 2026 issue of *Obstetrics & Gynecology*, the guideline sets out evidence-based recommendations for vasomotor symptoms such as hot flashes and night sweats, as well as sleep and mood symptoms associated with spontaneous or medically induced menopause from age 40 onwards.

The guideline is numbered 13 and replaces ACOG Practice Bulletin No. 141, which was published in January 2014. Its arrival matters because women are increasingly asking for clearer information about what can help, what the evidence supports and how treatment decisions should be made.

## What does the new guideline cover?

The document focuses on three symptom areas that can have a substantial effect on daily life:

- **Vasomotor symptoms:** hot flashes and night sweats that can interrupt work, sleep and social activities.
- **Sleep symptoms:** difficulties sleeping that may occur alongside the menopause transition.
- **Mood symptoms:** emotional and mood-related concerns associated with menopause.

ACOG says the guideline considers both pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions. Recommendations are graded according to the strength of the evidence, and the document also includes good-practice points where the evidence is not sufficient for a formal recommendation.

That distinction is important. Not every treatment has the same level of supporting evidence, and a guideline is not a one-size-fits-all prescription. The right approach depends on a woman's symptoms, medical history, preferences and individual risk factors.

## What does ACOG recommend for treatment?

The guideline recommends systemic menopausal hormone therapy for vasomotor symptoms in people without contraindications. For someone with a uterus, the regimen generally includes estrogen and a progestogen; estrogen alone may be used when endometrial protection is not needed. ACOG also recommends systemic hormone therapy for sleep disturbance associated with vasomotor symptoms in eligible individuals.

For people who do not want hormone therapy or have a contraindication, ACOG recommends SSRIs or SNRIs and neurokinin receptor antagonists for vasomotor symptoms and vasomotor-related sleep disturbance. Gabapentin is suggested when other recommended non-hormonal medicines are ineffective or poorly tolerated.

The guideline also suggests cognitive behavioural therapy for vasomotor symptoms, sleep disturbance and mood symptoms. Mindfulness-based interventions and physical exercise are suggested for sleep and mood symptoms. Clinical hypnosis and acupuncture receive conditional recommendations with lower-quality evidence.

These are guideline recommendations, not instructions for an individual reader to start a medicine or therapy. The strength of recommendation and evidence quality differ by intervention. Depression that meets criteria for major depressive disorder, or is the predominant symptom, should be assessed and treated using depression care guidance; hormone therapy should not be presented as a substitute for appropriate depression treatment. Persistent or unscheduled bleeding after starting hormone therapy needs clinical evaluation.

**Questions to ask your clinician**

1. Which of my symptoms are we trying to treat?
2. Am I eligible for hormone therapy, given my medical history?
3. If not, which evidence-based non-hormonal options are suitable?
4. What benefits should I expect, and what side effects should I watch for?
5. When should we review the treatment?

The guideline comes from a US professional organisation, so Indian clinicians generally interpret it alongside Indian Menopause Society guidance and local prescribing practice.

## Does this mean every woman should take hormone therapy?

No. A new guideline should not be read as an instruction for every woman to start menopausal hormone therapy.

Hormone therapy can be an effective option for some people with troublesome menopause symptoms, but it is not suitable for everyone. The decision requires a conversation with a qualified clinician about the benefits, possible risks, timing, type of treatment and personal medical history. Women should not start, stop or change prescribed hormones based on a headline or social media post. For how this decision is usually approached, see our guide on [whether to take oestrogen during perimenopause](/blog/should-i-take-estrogen-perimenopause).

There are also non-hormonal approaches and treatments. Their suitability depends on the symptom being treated and the evidence supporting each option. A clinician can help distinguish menopause-related symptoms from other conditions that may need separate assessment.

## Why this matters during perimenopause

Perimenopause is the transition before menopause. Hormone levels can fluctuate, periods may become less predictable, and symptoms can appear before menstruation stops completely. Some women experience hot flashes or sleep disruption; others first notice changes in mood, concentration or cycle patterns.

A guideline that addresses symptoms across the menopause transition can help make clinical conversations more structured. It may also help women understand that menopause care is not limited to managing periods or hot flashes.

## What should Indian women take away?

The ACOG guideline is an American clinical document, not an Indian government guideline. Its recommendations should not automatically be treated as local policy. However, it offers a useful reference point for understanding how evidence-based menopause care is being discussed internationally.

For women in India, the practical step is to record the symptoms that are affecting everyday life, when they occur, how they affect sleep or work, and any relevant medical history. Take those notes to a gynaecologist or another qualified healthcare professional. Ask what may be related to perimenopause, what else should be ruled out and which options are appropriate in your circumstances. Our overview of [hormonal and non-hormonal symptom treatments](/blog/hormonal-symptom-treatments-perimenopause) can help you prepare your questions.

The larger message is straightforward: women do not have to accept disruptive symptoms as something they must simply endure. They deserve a careful assessment and an informed discussion of available care.

## The bottom line

ACOG's November 2026 guideline updates clinical recommendations for menopause-related hot flashes, sleep and mood symptoms, replacing a 2014 practice bulletin. It does not prescribe one treatment for everyone. Its value lies in bringing current evidence into a more structured conversation between women and their clinicians.
