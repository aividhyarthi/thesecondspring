The longevity market is full of confident promises. A supplement may claim to slow ageing. A fasting routine may be promoted as a way to reset the body. A biological-age test may suggest that a person is getting younger. But what does the human evidence actually show?

A review published on 20 November 2026 in the journal *Herz* takes a useful approach to this question. Instead of treating every proposed longevity intervention as equally proven, it separates established cardiovascular benefits from ideas that remain hypothetical.

Its central message is important: nutrition can support a longer and healthier life by improving known risk factors, especially blood pressure, blood lipids and excess adiposity. But no specific diet, supplement or fasting protocol has been shown in a randomised human trial to extend maximum lifespan.

## What is the new review about?

The paper, *Nutrition, metabolism, and cardiovascular longevity: what is established, what is hypothesis?*, is a narrative review by A. Rossmeißl and K. Timper. It brings together evidence from randomised trials, cohort studies and mechanistic research on dietary patterns, energy balance, meal timing, food quality, supplements and biological-age markers.

A narrative review is not a new clinical trial. It assesses and discusses existing research. Its value lies in bringing different types of evidence together and identifying where claims are stronger than the data.

The authors focus on cardiovascular ageing because heart and blood vessel health are major parts of healthy ageing. This is a more useful way to discuss longevity than promising that one food or capsule can make people live to 100.

## What has stronger evidence?

The review identifies whole dietary patterns such as the Mediterranean diet and DASH diet, along with reducing excess sodium, as approaches with evidence for lowering cardiovascular risk and improving hard cardiovascular outcomes.

These patterns are not built around one miracle ingredient. They emphasise an overall way of eating, including vegetables, fruit, pulses, whole grains, nuts and appropriate sources of protein, while limiting excess salt and less healthy dietary patterns.

The authors also point to established cardiovascular risk factors such as blood pressure, apolipoprotein B-containing lipoproteins and excess adiposity. Managing these factors has a stronger evidence base than trying to change an experimental biological-age score.

For Indian readers, the practical lesson is not that everyone must follow a Mediterranean menu. Dietary patterns need to work with local food habits, affordability, culture, medical needs and access. Indian meals can include vegetables, pulses, whole grains, nuts and other nutritious foods. Portion sizes, cooking methods, salt intake and the person's overall health also matter.

## Does intermittent fasting slow ageing?

Intermittent fasting is often presented as a longevity shortcut. The review finds that the evidence is more limited than many claims suggest.

Time-restricted eating may help some people organise meals and reduce overall energy intake. But clinical trials have generally shown modest results. The review discusses evidence that time-restricted eating has not consistently produced additional cardiometabolic benefits beyond structured calorie restriction.

It also notes that some fasting studies have reported loss of lean mass. That matters particularly in midlife and older age, when maintaining muscle strength and function is an important part of healthy ageing.

The authors conclude that fasting should be viewed, at most, as one possible way for some people to manage eating patterns, not as a proven lifespan-extending intervention.

Fasting is not suitable for everyone. People with diabetes, those using glucose-lowering medicines, people with a history of eating disorders, frail older adults and people with certain medical conditions should seek individual medical advice before making major changes to meal timing or food intake.

## Are supplements proven to increase lifespan?

This is where the gap between marketing and evidence can become especially wide.

The review discusses vitamin D, omega-3 products, antioxidant vitamins and proposed geroprotectors such as metformin, rapamycin analogues, NAD+ precursors and spermidine. It notes that improvements in selected biomarkers do not automatically translate into fewer heart attacks, longer life or better day-to-day function.

The review reports that no dietary supplement has demonstrated an extension of human healthspan or lifespan in a randomised trial as of 2026. That does not mean supplements are never medically useful. A supplement may be appropriate to correct a diagnosed deficiency or meet a specific clinical need. That is different from taking it because it is advertised as an anti-ageing product.

People should not start prescription medicines such as metformin or rapamycin for longevity without a medical indication and specialist oversight.

## Why muscle matters in the longevity conversation

The review also raises a practical issue in nutritional care: preserving lean mass.

Weight reduction is not automatically a health success if it comes with substantial loss of muscle. This is relevant for people using weight-loss interventions, including medicines, and for older adults who may already be at risk of muscle loss.

A healthy ageing plan should consider strength, physical function, adequate nutrition and clinical context, not only body weight or a biological-age number. The review highlights the need to combine weight-management strategies with attention to lean mass and micronutrient adequacy.

## What about biological-age clocks?

Biological-age tests are attracting attention because they appear to turn ageing into a number. The review cautions that epigenetic clocks and other biomarkers are not yet established substitutes for clinical outcomes.

A marker can change without proving that a person will live longer or avoid disease. To demonstrate that an intervention improves longevity, researchers need trials that measure meaningful health outcomes over an appropriate period, not only short-term shifts in laboratory measures.

## What does this mean for people in India?

India has a high burden of diabetes, hypertension and cardiovascular disease. A longevity conversation that ignores these conditions risks becoming a lifestyle trend rather than useful health education.

A practical approach is to focus on what can be measured and managed: blood pressure, blood glucose where clinically indicated, cholesterol and overall cardiovascular risk. Food choices should support health while remaining realistic for the household. Physical activity and resistance exercise can help preserve function and muscle, while sleep, smoking, alcohol use and access to preventive care also influence health.

There is no need to buy an expensive longevity supplement or adopt an extreme fasting schedule to begin improving health habits. People with chronic conditions should discuss major dietary changes with their clinicians.

## The takeaway

The new review offers a useful test for longevity claims: ask whether the intervention has been shown to improve meaningful human health outcomes, or whether it has only changed a biomarker in a small or short study.

Whole dietary patterns and management of established cardiovascular risks have a stronger evidence base. Supplements, biological-age clocks and fasting as a lifespan-extending intervention remain unproven. Longevity is not a product category. It is the long-term result of many interacting health, social and medical factors.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can a diet make people live longer?**

Healthy dietary patterns can reduce cardiovascular risk and support health. This review does not establish that any specific diet extends maximum human lifespan.

**Is intermittent fasting proven to slow ageing?**

No. The review describes modest clinical results and no proof that a fasting protocol extends human lifespan.

**Do longevity supplements work?**

Some supplements have specific medical uses, such as correcting deficiencies. But no dietary supplement has demonstrated an extension of human healthspan or lifespan in a randomised trial as of 2026, according to the review.

**What is the strongest nutrition evidence for heart health?**

The review highlights whole dietary patterns such as Mediterranean and DASH diets, sodium reduction and management of established cardiovascular risk factors.

**Should people with diabetes try fasting?**

Not without individual medical advice. Meal timing can affect glucose levels and medication needs. People with diabetes should discuss fasting with their treating clinician.
