# Could Menopause Be Linked to Changes in Brain Ageing? New Study Offers Biological Clues

Brain fog, forgetfulness and difficulty concentrating are concerns many women raise during perimenopause and menopause. A study published in *Nature Medicine* on 22 September 2026 adds to the scientific discussion by examining blood proteins associated with menopause and biological ageing in the brain.

The researchers found menopause-associated changes in proteins linked to inflammatory, synaptic and metabolic processes. In a larger validation group, they also identified patterns associated with accelerated ageing measures, including brain ageing.

The findings offer biological clues. They do not establish that menopause causes dementia, nor do they mean that a woman experiencing brain fog is developing Alzheimer's disease.

## What did the researchers study?

The research team first examined blood protein data from 80 women aged 43 to 58 who had been carefully classified as premenopausal, perimenopausal or postmenopausal using the STRAW+10 reproductive staging system.

The researchers then tested their findings in a larger group of 2,814 age-matched women using a different blood protein platform. This second analysis helped assess whether the patterns identified in the smaller, carefully staged group could be observed in a much larger dataset.

The study reported menopause-associated differences in proteins involved in inflammation, synaptic biology and metabolism. Some of these patterns were linked to measures of organ and cell ageing, including biological measures related to the brain.

## What are blood proteins telling researchers?

Proteins help cells communicate and carry out essential functions. Measuring many proteins in blood can give researchers clues about biological processes taking place across the body.

In this study, the researchers used proteomics, the large-scale study of proteins, to explore how biological patterns differed across reproductive stages. They reported that some changes tracked more strongly with hormone levels than with chronological age in the initial group.

This does not mean a blood protein panel can currently diagnose menopause-related brain ageing in an individual woman. The study is a research analysis, not a clinical screening test.

## Does menopause increase dementia risk?

The study investigates biological links between menopause and processes relevant to brain ageing. It does not prove that menopause causes dementia or that every woman will experience accelerated brain ageing.

Dementia is complex. Age, genetics, cardiovascular health, metabolic conditions, lifestyle, education and other factors can all contribute to risk. A biological association in a research dataset is not the same as demonstrating that one event causes a later disease.

The distinction matters because women deserve accurate information, not fear. Brain fog during perimenopause can have several possible contributors, including disrupted sleep, mood symptoms, stress and other medical conditions. Persistent, worsening or concerning cognitive changes should be discussed with a healthcare professional rather than automatically attributed to hormones.

## Why the research matters for women in midlife

Menopause research has often focused on menstrual changes and hot flashes. Studies examining the brain, blood proteins and other biological systems broaden the questions researchers can ask about women's health.

For Indian women, the findings are a reason to take cognitive concerns seriously while avoiding unproven claims. A conversation with a clinician can include sleep quality, mood, menstrual changes, medicines, thyroid health, anaemia, blood pressure, blood sugar and other factors relevant to the individual.

There is no evidence from this study that women should undergo commercial proteomic testing or take a particular supplement to prevent brain ageing.

## What should happen next?

The findings need further validation in larger and more diverse populations, with studies that follow women over time. Researchers will need to establish which protein patterns are clinically meaningful, whether they predict future cognitive outcomes and whether any interventions can change those outcomes.

The study is an important step in understanding the biology of menopause. It is not the final word on brain health or dementia prevention.

## The bottom line

A 2026 Nature Medicine study found menopause-associated blood protein patterns linked to biological processes involved in inflammation, synaptic function, metabolism and brain ageing. It offers clues for future research, not a diagnosis or proof that menopause causes dementia.
