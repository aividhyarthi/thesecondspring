# WHO Highlights the Need for Better Research and Global Guidance on Early Menopause

When menopause symptoms begin in a woman's twenties or thirties, the experience can be confusing and isolating. Many people do not expect hot flashes, disrupted sleep or menstrual changes at that age. Even healthcare professionals may initially look for other explanations.

A World Health Organization (WHO) feature published on 16 October 2026 has brought attention to the research and care gaps affecting women who experience menopause unusually early. The article is based on an interview with physician and scientist Priya Satalkar-Götz, who describes experiencing her first symptoms in her mid-twenties and receiving a diagnosis at 33.

Her experience illustrates why early menopause needs to be recognised, assessed and supported rather than dismissed because of age.

## What does the WHO feature highlight?

Satalkar-Götz describes experiencing hot flashes during a work trip to India at the age of 26. Her menstrual cycles later became increasingly irregular, and she experienced night-time symptoms and sleep disruption. She says several consultations initially attributed her symptoms to stress, lifestyle changes or weight loss.

After moving to Switzerland, further assessment led to a diagnosis at age 33. She describes the emotional impact of receiving the diagnosis and the importance of finding a reproductive endocrinologist who listened and helped her consider treatment and longer-term health.

The WHO feature also highlights a wider problem: much menopause research has historically focused on women reaching menopause around the typical age. Women experiencing menopause much earlier may have different questions about fertility, bone health, treatment and long-term monitoring.

The article argues for high-quality long-term research, evidence that reflects different populations and better global guidance for healthcare professionals.

## Early menopause and premature ovarian insufficiency are not the same term

The language matters. Early menopause generally refers to menopause occurring between ages 40 and 45. Premature ovarian insufficiency (POI) refers to loss or impairment of ovarian function before age 40. These are related but distinct clinical terms, and assessment should be carried out by a qualified healthcare professional.

A woman experiencing irregular periods or hot flashes at a younger age should not assume that she has early menopause or POI. There can be several possible explanations, and a proper medical evaluation is important.

## Why early recognition matters

When ovarian function declines earlier than expected, women may have concerns that go beyond immediate symptoms. These can include fertility, bone health, cardiovascular health, sexual wellbeing and emotional adjustment.

The right care depends on the diagnosis and the person's circumstances. The WHO feature describes the value of a clinician who explains the available evidence, discusses options and includes the patient in decisions. It does not offer a single treatment plan for every woman.

## What does this mean for India?

The interview has a direct point of connection with India because Satalkar-Götz recalls her first hot flash while returning from a work trip to the country. But one personal experience cannot tell us how common early menopause or POI is among Indian women.

India needs more research into recognition, diagnosis, treatment access and the experiences of women who enter menopause earlier than expected. Awareness also needs to reach women outside major cities and those who may not have easy access to specialist reproductive healthcare.

Women with persistent menstrual changes, hot flashes or other symptoms at a younger age should seek medical assessment rather than self-diagnose. A clinician can review menstrual history, symptoms and relevant tests, and discuss whether specialist referral is needed.

## Why global guidance matters

The WHO feature explains that without clear evidence and guidance, clinicians may take very different approaches. A global guideline could help establish a trusted foundation while recognising that individual circumstances and health systems differ.

The article is an interview and feature story. It is not the publication of a new WHO clinical guideline. Its importance lies in drawing attention to the need for better evidence and more consistent care.

## The bottom line

WHO's October feature makes a case for stronger research and better guidance for women who experience menopause early. Its central message is that age should not be a reason to dismiss symptoms, and women deserve timely assessment, clear information and compassionate support.
