# For a Longer Life, Does Fitness Matter More Than Diet? What a New Review Says

Longevity advice often focuses on what to eat. But a review discussed in research coverage on 19 November 2026 asks us to pay closer attention to physical activity and cardiorespiratory fitness when thinking about how long people live.

The review, published in *Mayo Clinic Proceedings*, compared evidence relating diet quality, physical activity and fitness to longevity. Its broad message is that physical activity and fitness show particularly strong associations with survival in the evidence examined.

## What did the review report?

The research synthesis drew on existing studies and comparisons of health outcomes. One summary of the findings reported that people taking around 11,000 to 16,000 steps a day had substantially lower all-cause mortality than groups taking roughly 2,000 to 5,000 steps.

Those figures are comparisons between groups in the underlying evidence. They are not a prescription that every person must reach 11,000 steps, and they do not prove that increasing steps alone will produce the same reduction in an individual's risk.

## Why fitness matters

Cardiorespiratory fitness describes how effectively the heart, lungs and muscles work together during physical activity. It is influenced by genetics, health conditions and everyday movement, but it can also improve with suitable training.

Fitness matters because it reflects the body's ability to meet physical demands. It can support independence, mobility and the ability to carry out daily activities as people age.

## Does this mean diet is less important?

No. Nutrition remains essential for health. A balanced diet supports muscle, bone, immune function and metabolic health. Poor nutrition can increase the risk of deficiencies and chronic disease.

The review compares associations in existing evidence. It does not show that exercise can compensate for an unhealthy diet, or that diet quality does not matter. The strongest practical approach is to combine regular movement with adequate, varied nutrition.

## What can people in India do?

Start with activity that fits your health and daily routine. Walking is accessible for many people, but it is not the only option. Cycling, swimming, dancing, gardening and structured exercise can all contribute.

Strength activity is also important as people age because preserving muscle supports mobility and everyday function. People who are inactive, older, living with chronic illness or recovering from an injury may need to build activity gradually with professional guidance.

Air quality, heat, safe walking spaces, work hours and mobility can affect what is realistic in different Indian cities and villages. A sustainable routine is more useful than chasing a step target that does not fit someone's circumstances.

## The takeaway

The new review reinforces an important point: movement and fitness deserve a central place in healthy-ageing conversations. It does not create a contest between food and exercise. Both matter, and the right plan is one a person can sustain safely over time.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many steps should I walk daily to live longer?**

The review does not establish one ideal target for everyone. Start from your current activity level and increase gradually if it is safe for you.

**Is walking enough for healthy ageing?**

Walking can improve activity and cardiovascular fitness, but a broader routine that includes strength and balance work may offer additional benefits.

**Can exercise make up for a poor diet?**

No. Exercise and nutrition support health in different ways. Neither fully replaces the other.
