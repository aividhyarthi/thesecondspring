# Research Update Brief: Sleep Disruption During the Menopausal Transition

**Recommendation:** Update the two existing sleep articles rather than publish a third general sleep article. The repository already has separate coverage of insomnia and sleep apnoea.

## New research

Lyu AC and Kravitz HM. "Sleep Disruption in the Menopausal Transition and Postmenopause: A Narrative Review." *Obstetrics & Gynecology*. 2026;148(5):616-625. DOI: [10.1097/AOG.0000000000006420](https://doi.org/10.1097/AOG.0000000000006420).

The review searched literature published from January 2015 to March 2026. It discusses insomnia, obstructive sleep apnoea, restless legs syndrome and periodic limb movement disorder, as well as biological, psychological and social contributors to sleep disruption.

The authors emphasise that sleep disruption has multiple possible causes. Its prevalence varies according to menopausal stage, vasomotor symptoms and how sleep is measured.

## Suggested update to the insomnia article

Add a section explaining that not every night-time awakening is caused by hot flashes or hormone changes. Sleep disruption during midlife can involve:

- Insomnia, including difficulty falling asleep, staying asleep or waking too early.
- Obstructive sleep apnoea, which may involve snoring, gasping, witnessed pauses in breathing or unrefreshing sleep.
- Restless legs syndrome, which involves an urge to move the legs, often with uncomfortable sensations that are worse at rest and in the evening.
- Periodic limb movements during sleep, which may require specialist assessment to identify.
- Vasomotor symptoms, mood changes, health conditions, medicines and social stressors.

The review describes insomnia assessment using clinical history, a sleep questionnaire and a one-to-two-week sleep diary. Polysomnography is not routinely required for uncomplicated insomnia, but may be considered when another sleep disorder is suspected.

## Suggested update to the sleep apnoea article

The review notes that obstructive sleep apnoea can present differently in women. Alongside loud snoring, gasping and witnessed pauses in breathing, women may report daytime sleepiness, morning headaches, low energy, insomnia, nightmares, mood symptoms or memory difficulties.

These symptoms are not specific to sleep apnoea. They should not be used as a self-diagnosis checklist. If there is concern, a clinician can assess whether a sleep study or referral is appropriate.

## Evidence and uncertainty

| Supported by the review | Not established by the review |
|---|---|
| Sleep disruption during the menopause transition has multiple biological, psychological and social contributors. | That every sleep problem in midlife is caused by menopause. |
| Insomnia, obstructive sleep apnoea and restless legs syndrome are relevant conditions to consider. | That a woman with fatigue or brain fog necessarily has one of these disorders. |
| Sleep apnoea may be under-recognised when symptoms are attributed only to menopause. | That hormone therapy is a treatment for sleep apnoea. |
| Clinical history and appropriate assessment help distinguish sleep complaints. | That every woman with insomnia needs a sleep study. |

## India-specific editorial angle

Add a short section on practical barriers to sleep assessment in India, but do not make unsupported claims about prevalence or access. Explain that women can describe symptoms in their own words and mention snoring, gasping, leg discomfort, medicines, shift work, caregiving and daytime sleepiness during a consultation.

Avoid implying that sleep apnoea is caused by falling progesterone alone. The review presents a multifactorial picture.
