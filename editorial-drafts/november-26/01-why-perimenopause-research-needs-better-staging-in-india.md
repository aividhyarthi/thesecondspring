# Why Is Perimenopause So Difficult to Study and Recognise in India?

A woman in her early forties notices that her periods are changing. Some months they arrive earlier than expected. Other months the gap is longer. She is sleeping poorly, feels unusually tired and finds it harder to concentrate. She wonders whether this is perimenopause, stress, a thyroid problem or something else.

There is no single symptom that answers that question. And the research used to understand perimenopause has its own challenge: studies do not always classify the reproductive stages in the same way.

A review published online in *Climacteric* on 9 November 2026 examined how perimenopause has been studied in low- and middle-income countries. Its central finding is important for countries such as India: researchers have often used different methods to decide which women are in perimenopause. That makes it harder to compare studies and build evidence that can guide care.

## What did the 2026 review find?

The authors examined research on reproductive-stage classification in low- and middle-income countries. Of 288 full-text articles assessed, 90 studies published between 2005 and June 2026 were included.

The review found considerable variation in the way studies identified reproductive stages:

- 20% used the Stages of Reproductive Aging Workshop +10 framework, commonly called STRAW+10.
- 21% used the earlier STRAW framework.
- 10% used World Health Organization criteria.
- 48.9% relied on menstrual history without explicitly referring to a recognised staging framework.

Most included studies were cross-sectional. Only seven studies, or 7.8%, were longitudinal, and four, or 4.4%, were nationally representative.

These figures describe the studies included in the review. They are not estimates of how many Indian women experience perimenopause, nor do they measure how often Indian clinicians diagnose it correctly.

## Why does the way researchers classify perimenopause matter?

Perimenopause is a transition, not a single event. Menstrual-cycle patterns change over time, and symptoms may begin before a woman knows that she has entered the transition.

Researchers need a consistent way to describe reproductive stages. Without it, two studies may use the same word, perimenopause, while including women identified in quite different ways.

That creates several problems.

**Studies become difficult to compare.** A study that identifies women using menstrual-cycle changes may not be directly comparable with one that relies only on a participant saying she is perimenopausal.

**Symptoms can be attributed to the wrong stage.** If reproductive stage is not classified consistently, researchers may not know whether a finding is associated with early transition, late transition or postmenopause.

**Local evidence becomes harder to interpret.** Indian studies need to describe who was included, how reproductive stage was assessed and which population the results represent.

**Clinical recommendations have a weaker local evidence base.** Research can inform care only when the population and methods are clear enough to understand what the findings apply to.

## What is STRAW+10?

STRAW+10 is an internationally recognised framework for describing reproductive ageing. It uses menstrual-cycle patterns and, where appropriate, supportive hormonal information to classify reproductive stages.

It is a research and staging framework. It should not be presented as a simple home test or a substitute for clinical assessment.

The review argues that wider use of standardised frameworks such as STRAW+10 could improve the quality and comparability of research in low- and middle-income countries. The authors also recognise that researchers must consider practical and contextual challenges when applying such frameworks.

## What does this mean for women in India?

The review is about low- and middle-income countries as a group. It does not establish that every Indian study has used inconsistent methods, and it does not measure the accuracy of diagnosis in Indian hospitals.

But it raises questions that matter for Indian research and healthcare.

India is not one uniform population. Women live in different regions, speak different languages, have different access to healthcare and may have very different experiences of work, nutrition, chronic illness and family responsibilities. Studies need to describe these differences rather than treating all midlife women as one group.

A stronger Indian evidence base would include women from different states, income groups, rural and urban settings, and social backgrounds. It would follow women over time, record menstrual patterns carefully and explain how reproductive stage was determined.

## Does a changing period mean you are in perimenopause?

Not necessarily. Changes in menstrual timing can occur during the menopause transition, but they can also have other causes. Thyroid conditions, pregnancy, some medicines, uterine conditions and other health issues may affect bleeding patterns.

A clinician can assess menstrual history, age, symptoms and relevant medical history. Testing is guided by the individual situation. There is no single blood test that confirms perimenopause in every woman.

Very heavy bleeding, bleeding between periods, bleeding after sex or bleeding after menopause should not automatically be attributed to perimenopause. These symptoms deserve medical assessment.

## What better Indian perimenopause research should look like

The review points towards a practical research agenda:

1. Use clearly described and consistent reproductive-stage criteria.
2. Follow women over time instead of relying only on one-time surveys.
3. Include participants from varied regions, income groups and rural and urban settings.
4. Record menstrual patterns and symptoms using transparent methods.
5. Distinguish self-reported symptoms from clinically assessed conditions.
6. Publish enough detail for other researchers to compare and reproduce the work.

These steps will not solve every gap in menopause care. They can, however, make the evidence more useful.

## The takeaway

A major challenge in perimenopause research is not simply a lack of studies. It is that studies do not always define the reproductive stages in the same way.

A 2026 review found substantial variation in staging methods across research in low- and middle-income countries. For India, the lesson is to build better local evidence using clear methods and diverse populations.

Women should not have to become research experts to have their symptoms taken seriously. But better research can help clinicians, health systems and policymakers understand the transition more accurately.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is STRAW+10 a test for perimenopause?

No. It is a framework for classifying reproductive stages, mainly using menstrual-cycle patterns and supportive information where appropriate. It is not a home diagnostic test.

### Does this review prove that perimenopause is underdiagnosed in India?

No. It examined research methods in low- and middle-income countries. It did not measure diagnostic rates or diagnostic accuracy in India.

### Can a blood test confirm perimenopause?

There is no single blood test that confirms perimenopause in every woman. Assessment depends on age, menstrual history, symptoms and clinical context.

### Why do Indian studies need to include different communities?

Women’s health experiences and access to care vary by geography, income, work, health conditions and other factors. Diverse studies are needed before findings can be applied confidently across the country.
