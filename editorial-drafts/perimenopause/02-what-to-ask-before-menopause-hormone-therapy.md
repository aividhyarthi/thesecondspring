# What Should You Ask Your Doctor Before Starting Menopause Hormone Therapy?

A woman experiencing frequent hot flashes or night sweats may hear very different things about hormone therapy. One person may be told it is an effective option. Another may be advised to avoid it because of a previous illness or a particular risk factor. Both conversations may be appropriate because treatment decisions depend on the individual.

A paper published online on 24 November 2026 in *Menopause* describes a proposed framework called the Menopause Medical Eligibility Criteria, or MenoMEC. It aims to help clinicians consider hormone and non-hormone treatments for moderate to severe vasomotor symptoms while taking a woman's medical history into account.

The paper is useful because it focuses on a practical problem: choosing a treatment is not simply a matter of matching a symptom to a medicine.

## What is MenoMEC?

The researchers used a modified Delphi process involving 15 multidisciplinary experts. The panel reviewed proposed recommendations and rated their agreement. A threshold of at least 70% agreement was used to define consensus. Topics without sufficient agreement were discussed further before the recommendations were refined.

The framework considers treatment options alongside conditions that may affect eligibility or safety. These include cardiovascular disease, metabolic syndrome, a history of thrombosis, liver disease, smoking and hormone-sensitive cancers.

The panel supported hormone therapy as a first-line option for women who are eligible and non-hormonal treatment for women who have contraindications or do not wish to use hormone therapy. The paper also discusses non-hormonal medicines, including SSRIs or SNRIs, gabapentin and oxybutynin, as well as newer neurokinin-targeted medicines. Suitability depends on the person's circumstances and the medicine's own risks and limitations.

## Does this mean hormone therapy is right for every woman?

No. A consensus framework is not a personal prescription. It does not remove the need for a proper medical history, examination where appropriate, discussion of benefits and risks, and follow-up.

Hormone therapy is not suitable for everyone. The reasons may include a woman's medical history, unexplained vaginal bleeding, certain cancers, previous blood clots, cardiovascular events or liver disease. The exact assessment depends on the clinical context and the type of treatment being considered.

A woman should not start, stop or change hormone therapy because of a news story, an online checklist or a general recommendation.

## Seven useful questions to take to an appointment

You do not need to ask every question at once. These can help make the discussion more specific.

1. **Are my symptoms likely to be related to perimenopause or menopause?** Ask whether another condition or medicine could be contributing.
2. **What are the treatment options for the symptoms affecting me most?** Hot flashes, sleep disruption and mood symptoms may need different approaches.
3. **Does my medical history change which treatments are suitable?** Mention previous blood clots, heart or liver conditions, cancer history, smoking, migraine, medicines and any unexplained bleeding.
4. **If hormone therapy is considered, which formulation and route are being discussed, and why?** Ask how the options differ and what monitoring is needed.
5. **If hormone therapy is not suitable or I do not want it, what non-hormonal options are available?** Ask about expected benefits, side effects, interactions and cost.
6. **How will we know whether the treatment is helping?** Agree on which symptoms to track and when to review the plan.
7. **What symptoms or side effects should prompt me to contact the clinic?** Ask for clear instructions rather than relying on internet lists.

## What is important about the new paper?

MenoMEC is a US-focused expert consensus framework. It is not a randomised clinical trial and does not prove that one treatment is superior for every person with a particular condition. The publication also reports financial relationships among some authors and organisations, including involvement of Bayer in funding the research process. This should be visible to readers when interpreting the paper.

The framework may help clinicians organise complex decisions, but its recommendations should not be treated as automatically applicable to India. Drug availability, approvals, costs, local guidance and access to specialist care can differ.

India already has its own 2026 menopause clinical practice guidance from the Indian Menopause Society. Indian women should ask their treating clinician how local guidance and their personal health history apply to the options being discussed.

## A treatment conversation should include the woman's priorities

Some women want relief from night sweats because they are exhausted at work. Others are most troubled by sleep, mood, vaginal discomfort or the effect of symptoms on relationships. A useful consultation makes space for these priorities.

It should also explain what a treatment is expected to improve, what it is not expected to do, how long a trial may take, what the possible side effects are and when the decision will be reviewed.

If a woman feels rushed, she can ask for the benefits, risks and alternatives to be explained in plain language. Seeking a second opinion from a qualified menopause clinician may be reasonable when the decision is complex.

## The bottom line

The new MenoMEC paper reinforces an important principle: menopause treatment should be individualised. Hormone therapy can be appropriate for some women, while non-hormonal options may be more suitable for others. The right decision depends on symptoms, medical history, preferences, access and a clinician's assessment.

The best appointment is not one in which a woman is handed a universal answer. It is one in which she understands the options and takes part in the decision.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is MenoMEC an official Indian treatment guideline?

No. The paper describes a US-focused expert consensus framework. It should not replace Indian clinical guidance or a consultation with a qualified clinician.

### Does the paper say hormone therapy is always the first choice?

It supports hormone therapy as a first-line option for women who are eligible. It also recognises non-hormonal options for those with contraindications or who do not want hormone therapy.

### Can I use this article to decide whether I should start HRT?

No. Use the questions to prepare for a medical appointment. Treatment eligibility and choice require individual clinical assessment.
