# Does Your Weight Affect How Severe Perimenopause Symptoms Become?

A new study involving nearly 2,000 women suggests that body weight may be one part of the perimenopause symptom picture. But the findings are more nuanced than the familiar advice to simply lose weight.

Women in their late 30s and 40s can experience hot flashes, night sweats, body aches, sleep disruption, mood changes and shifts in their periods. Some have a difficult transition. Others have relatively mild symptoms. Researchers are still trying to understand why that experience differs so much from one woman to another.

A study published online on 24 November 2026 in the journal *Menopause* examined whether body mass index, or BMI, was associated with the severity of symptoms during perimenopause. The researchers analysed data from 1,988 women aged 35 and older who were identified as being in perimenopause through the Flo app.

## What did the researchers study?

The team compared BMI categories with scores on the Menopause Rating Scale. This scale measures symptoms across three broad areas: psychological, physical or somatic, and urogenital.

The researchers used statistical models that accounted for several factors, including age, race and ethnicity, income, education, smoking, physical activity, alcohol use and certain health conditions such as anxiety, depression and diabetes.

The study found that obesity classes I, II and III were associated with higher overall symptom scores and higher physical symptom scores compared with the normal-weight reference group. The association was not independently seen for psychological or urogenital symptom scores after the adjustments described in the paper.

That distinction matters. The study does not say that every woman with a higher BMI will have more severe symptoms, or that BMI explains why a particular woman is struggling.

## Weight was not the strongest factor in every symptom area

One of the more important findings was the role of mental health and social circumstances. Anxiety and depression were among the strongest independent predictors of symptom burden across the symptom domains examined. Lower income, lower educational attainment and active smoking were also associated with higher symptom scores. More frequent physical activity was associated with lower symptom burden.

This is a useful reminder that perimenopause does not happen in isolation. A woman may be navigating hormonal changes while also dealing with work pressure, caring responsibilities, financial stress, poor sleep or an existing health condition.

It would be misleading to reduce all of that to a number on a weighing scale.

## Does the study prove that losing weight will reduce symptoms?

No. This was a cross-sectional study. It examined associations at a point in time rather than following women to see what happened when their weight changed.

It cannot establish whether higher BMI contributes to symptoms, whether symptoms influence activity and weight, or whether other factors affect both. It also cannot show that a weight-loss programme would reduce hot flashes, aches or the overall symptom score.

The findings should not be used to blame women for their symptoms or to recommend restrictive diets, supplements or medication.

## Why might body weight and physical symptoms be connected?

There are several possible explanations, but the study does not prove a specific biological pathway. Body fat can influence heat regulation and metabolic health. Sleep, physical activity, insulin sensitivity, inflammation and other health conditions may also be relevant. Hormonal fluctuations during the menopause transition add another layer.

Researchers need longitudinal studies and intervention trials to understand which relationships are causal and whether changing a modifiable factor changes symptoms.

## What should women in India take from this research?

The study was global, but it does not provide a specific Indian estimate or establish that the same associations apply equally across every population. Indian women also differ in diet, activity patterns, healthcare access, body composition and the age at which they experience the menopause transition. Local studies are needed.

For an individual woman, the useful next step is a whole-health conversation rather than a weight-only conversation. Depending on her history, a clinician may discuss blood pressure, blood glucose, cholesterol, sleep, mood, physical activity, menstrual changes and other symptoms.

If movement has become difficult because of pain, fatigue or poor sleep, that deserves attention in its own right. A clinician or qualified physiotherapist can help identify an appropriate starting point. There is no single target weight that can be prescribed from this study.

## The practical takeaway

Higher BMI was associated with greater overall and physical symptom burden in this study, but it was only one part of a much wider picture. Anxiety, depression, socioeconomic factors, smoking and physical activity also mattered.

The message is not that women must lose weight to get through perimenopause. It is that symptom care should look at the whole person, including physical health, mental health and the circumstances in which she lives.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does a higher BMI mean I will have worse perimenopause symptoms?

No. The study found a group-level association, not a prediction for an individual woman. Experiences vary, and BMI does not explain every symptom.

### Did the study find that BMI was linked to every type of symptom?

No. After adjustment, the association was seen for overall and physical symptom scores, but not independently for psychological or urogenital scores.

### Will losing weight stop hot flashes?

This study did not test weight loss and cannot answer that question. Do not treat weight loss as a proven treatment for hot flashes on the basis of this research.

### What should I discuss with my doctor?

Discuss the symptoms affecting your daily life, menstrual changes, sleep, mood, existing health conditions and any concerns about weight or metabolic health. Ask which checks are appropriate for your own history.
