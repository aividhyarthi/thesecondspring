# Does Modern Menopause Hormone Therapy Increase Heart Risk? What a 52,000-Woman Study Found

For many women considering menopause hormone therapy, one question can feel more important than all the others: what does it mean for my heart?

A large study involving 52,330 women in Israel has added new information to that discussion. Presented at the American Heart Association's Scientific Sessions 2026, the study reported that women aged 45 to 59 who started contemporary hormone therapy did not have higher observed rates of heart attack, stroke, blood clots or death than a matched group who did not start treatment.

The result is interesting. It is not a guarantee that hormone therapy is safe for every woman, and it does not overturn the need to assess individual cardiovascular risk.

There is another important detail: the findings were presented as a conference research abstract. The American Heart Association says conference abstracts are not peer-reviewed full research papers and should be considered preliminary until a full manuscript is published.

## What did the study examine?

Researchers used health records from Clalit Health Services in Israel to compare 52,330 women aged 45 to 59. The women had no recorded history of heart disease, blood clots or cancer at the start of the analysis.

About half had started contemporary hormone therapy. The comparison group had visited a gynaecologist but had not started hormone therapy. The researchers matched the groups on several factors, including age, body mass index, blood pressure, cholesterol, diabetes, smoking and socioeconomic status.

The study included oral and transdermal treatment, including patches and gels. The average follow-up was 12 years, with health records extending as long as 23 years.

## What were the reported results?

The American Heart Association release reported the following:

| Outcome | Started hormone therapy | Did not start |
|---|---:|---:|
| Heart attack, stroke or related heart disease | 2,311 women (4.4%) | 2,383 women (4.6%) |
| Death before age 80 | 659 women (1.3%) | 653 women (1.2%) |
| Blood clot | 174 women (0.33%) | 175 women (0.33%) |

The researchers reported that the results were similar across the hormone therapy routes included in the analysis.

These are observed rates in the study groups. They should not be read as an individual's personal risk estimate or as proof that treatment caused no harm.

## What does the study tell us?

The findings are reassuring in one limited sense: in this selected group of women aged 45 to 59, with no recorded history of cardiovascular disease, blood clots or cancer, starting the contemporary hormone therapy examined was not associated with higher rates of the outcomes reported during follow-up.

The study also adds to the discussion about the timing and type of hormone therapy. It is relevant to women and clinicians who are trying to understand how evidence from older hormone therapy trials relates to contemporary formulations and routes.

But the study does not answer every question about hormone therapy and cardiovascular health.

## What does it not prove?

There are several important limitations.

**It was observational.** Researchers compared existing health records. They did not randomly assign women to take or avoid hormone therapy. Differences between the groups may remain even after statistical matching.

**The women were selected.** The analysis excluded women with a recorded history of heart disease, blood clots or cancer. The results cannot simply be applied to women with those conditions or other contraindications.

**The treatment could change over time.** The AHA summary notes that the analysis grouped women by the initial type of hormone therapy and did not capture every change in formulation or route during follow-up.

**Some relevant information may be missing.** The researchers noted that records may not fully capture differences in exercise, diet, other health conditions or the severity of menopause symptoms.

**The findings are preliminary.** A conference abstract is not the same as a full peer-reviewed paper. Detailed methods, analyses and independent scrutiny matter.

For these reasons, the correct interpretation is "no increased risk was observed in this selected observational cohort", not "HRT has no heart risk".

## Does this mean women should take HRT to protect their hearts?

No. The study does not show that hormone therapy prevents heart disease, and it is not a recommendation to start treatment for cardiovascular protection.

Menopause hormone therapy may be considered for appropriate menopause symptoms after an individual assessment of benefits and risks. It should not be started solely to prevent heart attack or stroke.

Women with existing heart disease, previous blood clots, certain cancers, unexplained bleeding or other relevant conditions need a careful clinical assessment. The choice of formulation, route and dose also matters and should be discussed with a qualified clinician.

Do not start, stop or change prescribed hormone therapy because of a headline or conference report.

## What should Indian women take from this?

The study was conducted in Israel and included a selected group of women. It does not provide a direct estimate of risk for Indian women, whose health profiles, access to care and treatment patterns may differ.

For an Indian woman considering hormone therapy, the practical next step is to discuss her own symptoms and medical history with a qualified gynaecologist or menopause specialist. Depending on her history, the conversation may include blood pressure, cholesterol, blood glucose, smoking, migraine, previous clots, cardiovascular conditions, cancer history and family history.

A clinician can explain whether hormone therapy is appropriate, what alternatives exist and how treatment should be reviewed. The study should inform that conversation, not make the decision.

## The bottom line

A 52,330-woman observational study presented at the American Heart Association's 2026 Scientific Sessions found no higher observed rates of heart attack, stroke, blood clots or death among selected women aged 45 to 59 who started contemporary hormone therapy.

That is a useful research signal, not a final answer. The findings are preliminary, the study was not randomised, and the participants did not represent women with a history of heart disease, clots or cancer. Until the full paper is peer-reviewed and published, the safest interpretation is cautious interest rather than a change in treatment advice.

## Frequently asked questions

### Did the study prove that modern HRT is safe for the heart?

No. It was an observational study presented as a conference abstract. It found no increased rate of the outcomes measured in the selected study population, but it cannot prove cause and effect or establish safety for every woman.

### How many women were included?

The study included 52,330 women aged 45 to 59 in Israel.

### Were women with previous heart disease included?

The AHA summary says the study population had no history of heart disease, blood clots or cancer at the start. The findings should not be generalised to women with those histories.

### Should I start hormone therapy to prevent heart disease?

No. This study does not show that hormone therapy prevents heart disease. Decisions about treatment for menopause symptoms should be made with a qualified clinician after reviewing personal risks and benefits.
