# Can High Blood Pressure Affect When Menopause Begins? A South Asian Study Offers a Clue

Menopause is usually discussed as a reproductive milestone. But the timing of menopause may also carry information about a woman's wider health. A new prospective study of South Asian women has examined whether cardiometabolic conditions during the reproductive years are associated with when natural menopause occurs. One finding stood out: women with hypertension reached natural menopause earlier, on average, than women without hypertension.

The result is important because South Asian populations experience a substantial burden of cardiometabolic risk. It also gives clinicians and women another reason to take blood pressure seriously before the menopause transition. But the study does not establish that high blood pressure causes menopause to happen earlier. That distinction matters.

## What did the researchers investigate?

The study, titled *Cardiometabolic dysfunction and age at natural menopause: a prospective study*, used longitudinal data from the Centre for Cardiometabolic Risk Reduction in South Asia (CARRS) cohort. Researchers analysed 2,225 menstruating women aged 30 to 50 at baseline. The data covered follow-up between 2010 and 2024.

The researchers examined four cardiometabolic measures: overweight or obesity, hypertension, diabetes and non-HDL cholesterol. They then assessed how these baseline conditions related to the timing of natural menopause.

The women had a median age of 37 years at baseline. During follow-up, 44% reached natural menopause. Among those women, the median age at menopause was 46 years.

## The main finding: hypertension was associated with earlier menopause

Women with hypertension reached natural menopause approximately one year earlier than women without hypertension. In the adjusted analysis, hypertension was associated with a higher rate of reaching natural menopause during follow-up. The reported hazard ratio was 1.34, with a 95% confidence interval of 1.19 to 1.52.

A hazard ratio is a comparison of the rate at which an event occurs over time. It is not the same as saying that every woman with hypertension will reach menopause one year earlier. Nor does it predict an individual's menopause date.

The researchers did not find a similar difference in menopause timing based on baseline diabetes, weight status or non-HDL cholesterol in this analysis. This does not mean those conditions are harmless. It means that this particular study did not find a statistically significant association between those baseline measures and menopause timing.

## Why might blood pressure and menopause timing be connected?

The study identifies an association. It does not settle the biological explanation.

Blood pressure, vascular health, inflammation, metabolic regulation and reproductive ageing may influence one another. It is possible that shared biological or social factors contribute to both hypertension and the timing of menopause. It is also possible that pathways involving vascular function are relevant. These are explanations to investigate, not conclusions established by this study.

The research should not be simplified into a headline claiming that hypertension brings on menopause. A prospective cohort can track health measures over time and identify patterns, but it cannot by itself prove that one condition causes another.

## Why the South Asian context matters

The study is particularly relevant because it examined women from a South Asian cohort rather than relying only on data from Europe or North America. South Asian populations face a high burden of cardiometabolic risk, often at younger ages and at body mass index levels that may be lower than thresholds commonly used in some Western populations.

The study's median menopause age of 46 among women who reached natural menopause during follow-up should not be treated as a universal estimate for every South Asian woman. The cohort has its own age distribution, recruitment and follow-up characteristics. Menopause timing varies between individuals and populations.

For Indian readers, the practical message is not to predict menopause from a blood pressure reading. It is to avoid treating blood pressure as a concern only for older adults. Regular measurement and appropriate clinical follow-up matter during the reproductive years and through midlife.

## What should women do with this information?

This paper does not test a blood pressure treatment programme and does not show that lowering blood pressure delays menopause. Women should not start, stop or change medication based on the study.

A sensible response is to know your blood pressure, discuss persistently raised readings with a qualified clinician, and assess other cardiovascular risk factors as part of routine care. A single elevated reading is not enough to diagnose hypertension. Measurement technique, repeat readings and the person's overall clinical context matter.

Women approaching perimenopause should also avoid assuming that every change in sleep, mood, menstrual pattern or energy is caused by blood pressure. Menstrual changes deserve their own assessment, especially if bleeding is unusually heavy, prolonged, occurs after menopause or is otherwise concerning.

## What the study does not tell us

There are several limits readers should keep in mind:

- The finding is observational. It shows an association, not proof of cause and effect.
- The analysis used baseline cardiometabolic measurements. Health status can change during a long follow-up period.
- The result does not allow an individual woman to calculate her menopause age.
- The absence of an association for diabetes, weight or non-HDL cholesterol in this analysis does not remove the established importance of managing those conditions.
- The study does not demonstrate that blood pressure treatment changes the timing of menopause.

## The takeaway

This South Asian study adds useful evidence to a field where local population data are important. Hypertension was associated with earlier natural menopause in the cohort, while the other baseline cardiometabolic measures examined were not associated with timing in the same way. The next step is to understand the mechanisms and whether the relationship is seen in other populations and settings.

For women, the immediate message is straightforward: blood pressure deserves attention well before old age. But this study is a reason to improve cardiovascular awareness, not a reason to fear menopause or alter treatment without medical advice.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does high blood pressure cause early menopause?
This study found an association between hypertension and earlier natural menopause. It did not prove that high blood pressure causes menopause to occur earlier.

### How many women were included?
The researchers analysed 2,225 South Asian women aged 30 to 50 at baseline, followed using CARRS cohort data from 2010 to 2024.

### How much earlier was menopause among women with hypertension?
The study reported that women with hypertension reached natural menopause approximately one year earlier on average. This is a group-level finding, not an individual prediction.

### Did diabetes or obesity also affect menopause timing?
The study did not find a difference in menopause timing based on baseline diabetes, weight or non-HDL cholesterol. This should not be interpreted as evidence that these conditions do not affect health.

### Should women take blood pressure medicines to delay menopause?
No. The study did not test whether blood pressure treatment delays menopause. Medication decisions should be made with a qualified clinician for established health reasons.
