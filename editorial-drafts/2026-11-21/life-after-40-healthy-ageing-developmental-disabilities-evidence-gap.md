# Who Gets Left Out of Longevity Research? A Review Finds Gaps in Healthy Ageing Trials

Longevity is often discussed through the experiences of people who have access to preventive healthcare, fitness programmes, nutrition advice and regular medical monitoring. But healthy ageing is not equally studied across all groups. Adults with developmental disabilities can face a higher burden of health problems and shorter life expectancy, yet the research on interventions that support their ageing needs is limited.

A new participatory scoping review published in *The Lancet Healthy Longevity* on 19 November 2026 examined randomised controlled trials of healthy ageing interventions involving adults with developmental disabilities. The review included 79 trials and 8,221 participants. It found that many studies addressed functional ability, but fewer incorporated life-course transitions, multiple levels of support, participatory approaches or strengths-based principles.

The findings raise a wider question for public health: are healthy ageing programmes designed around the lives and priorities of the people who need them?

## What did the researchers review?

The paper, *Closing the evidence gap: a life course and neurodiversity-informed participatory scoping review of healthy ageing randomised controlled trials in adults with developmental disabilities*, used a participatory approach informed by the World Health Organization's life-course framework and neurodiversity principles.

The researchers searched medical and scientific databases in June 2024 and April 2025. They included 79 randomised controlled trials involving 8,221 participants.

A scoping review maps the available research and identifies gaps. It is not the same as a meta-analysis that combines trial results to estimate one overall treatment effect. The review therefore tells us about the scope and design of the research, not that one specific intervention has been proven to extend life.

## What did the review find?

The review found that 77 of the 79 studies, or 97%, addressed at least one aspect of functional ability. That is a positive starting point because maintaining function is central to healthy ageing.

But the researchers identified gaps in other areas:

- 25 studies, or 32%, targeted life-course transitions.
- 28 studies, or 35%, addressed multiple levels of the ecosystem around a person.
- 22 studies, or 28%, used participatory approaches.
- 20 studies, or 25%, used strengths-based approaches.

These figures suggest that research has often focused on an individual's function without consistently examining the wider environment, transitions across life, participation in decisions or personal strengths.

## What do these gaps mean in everyday life?

Healthy ageing is not only about preventing disease. It also involves maintaining the ability to do things that matter to a person, participate in family and community life, make choices and receive support that respects their needs.

For adults with developmental disabilities, health and support needs can change as they move through adulthood and into older age. Families, carers, health professionals, social services and community organisations may all play a role.

A programme that focuses only on an individual's exercise or daily function may miss barriers in transport, accessible communication, housing, social participation or the availability of appropriate health services.

The review's use of life-course and neurodiversity principles draws attention to these wider factors. It encourages researchers to consider people as participants in their own health decisions, rather than only as recipients of services.

## Why participatory research matters

Participatory approaches involve people with relevant lived experience in shaping research questions, methods and interpretation. In this context, that means including adults with developmental disabilities in decisions about what healthy ageing should mean and which outcomes matter.

A strengths-based approach also matters. It recognises abilities, preferences, relationships and sources of support rather than defining people only by limitations or diagnoses.

The review found that these approaches were not consistently included in the trials it mapped. This is an opportunity for future research to be more inclusive from the beginning.

## Why this matters for India

The review is international and does not provide an estimate of the situation in India. It should not be used to claim that Indian services have the same gaps or that the trial findings directly represent Indian adults.

However, the questions are relevant to Indian public health and disability inclusion. Healthy ageing services need to consider accessibility, continuity of care, family and caregiver support, community participation and communication that works for people with different needs.

India's ageing and disability policies, health systems and community programmes can benefit from asking whether adults with developmental disabilities are included in planning and evaluation. The review provides a framework for asking better questions, not a ready-made local solution.

## What should future research do?

The authors point to opportunities to design interventions that address transitions across the life course, multiple levels of the ecosystem, participation and strengths.

Future trials could involve people with developmental disabilities in setting priorities, selecting outcomes and evaluating whether programmes are practical and acceptable. They could also examine how health services, families and communities work together to support wellbeing.

The goal is not simply to add more participants to existing trials. It is to ensure that research reflects the lives, choices and priorities of the people it is intended to serve.

## The takeaway

The review does not identify one intervention that makes people with developmental disabilities live longer. Instead, it identifies a gap in how healthy ageing research is designed. Although most of the trials addressed at least one aspect of function, fewer included life-course, ecosystem, participatory and strengths-based approaches.

A meaningful longevity agenda must ask not only how long people live, but whether they can age with dignity, choice, participation and appropriate support. That principle should apply to everyone.

## Frequently asked questions

### What are developmental disabilities?
The term refers to a group of conditions that begin during the developmental period and can affect physical, learning, language or behavioural functioning. The specific definition and eligibility criteria vary across research and services.

### How many studies were included in this review?
The researchers included 79 randomised controlled trials involving 8,221 adults with developmental disabilities.

### Did the review prove that a particular healthy ageing programme works?
No. It was a scoping review that mapped trials and assessed how they incorporated different healthy ageing principles. It did not establish one intervention as superior.

### What is a strengths-based approach?
It focuses on a person's abilities, preferences, relationships and resources, rather than defining them only by limitations or support needs.

### Does this research include Indian participants?
The review does not provide an India-specific estimate. Its findings can inform questions for Indian policy and research, but local studies are needed.
