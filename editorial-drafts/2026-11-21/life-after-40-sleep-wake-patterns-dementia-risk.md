# Sleep Is More Than Hours: A New Study Links Sleep-Wake Patterns with Dementia Risk

When people talk about sleep and brain health, the discussion often begins and ends with one number: how many hours a person sleeps. A new study suggests that the wider pattern may matter too. Researchers examined sleep, daytime activity, rest-activity rhythms and chronotype together, then followed older adults for more than a decade to assess dementia and cognitive outcomes.

The study, published online in *JAMA Network Open* on 18 November 2026, included 3,973 adults aged 60 and older from the UK-based Whitehall II cohort. It found that some combinations of disrupted daily rhythms, low physical activity and poor or restless sleep were associated with a higher risk of dementia.

The findings do not show that a particular bedtime prevents dementia. They do suggest that sleep should be understood as part of a wider daily rhythm that includes movement, time spent sitting and regularity.

## What did the researchers measure?

The researchers used accelerometer data collected during 2012 and 2013. They examined several dimensions of the sleep-wake cycle, including rest-activity rhythm, daytime activity, sleep characteristics and chronotype.

Rather than studying each measure in isolation, the team grouped participants into nine sleep-wake profiles based on 36 measures. They then examined the association between those profiles and later dementia diagnoses and cognitive change.

The analysis included 3,973 participants who were free of dementia at baseline and had valid accelerometer data and other required information. Their median age was 68.4 years, and 26% were women. Researchers identified 379 dementia cases over a median follow-up of 12.3 years.

## What did the study find?

Three profiles characterised by poorer rest-activity rhythms, together with other features such as low physical activity, poor sleep, restless sleep or high sedentary time, were associated with higher dementia risk or faster cognitive decline.

Compared with a reference group with a robust daily activity rhythm, light-intensity activity and good sleep, two profiles had higher dementia risk. One profile combined poorer rest-activity rhythms, low activity and poor sleep. Another included restless sleep and a weaker activity rhythm.

The reported hazard ratios for these two profiles were 1.94 and 1.96 respectively. These figures describe relative rates in the study's statistical models. They do not mean that an individual person's risk is automatically doubled, and they should not be interpreted as a prediction for any reader.

The researchers also found that some profiles were associated with faster cognitive decline over ten years. The results suggest that the combination of daily rhythm, movement and sleep may provide more information than sleep duration alone.

## Why daily rhythm and activity may matter

The body follows internal timing systems that influence sleep, alertness, hormone release, temperature and other functions. Daylight, physical activity, meals and regular routines can all interact with these rhythms.

A person who sleeps poorly, spends much of the day sitting and has an irregular daily routine may have a different overall pattern from someone who sleeps for a similar number of hours but remains active and has a stable daily rhythm.

The study does not establish which part of the pattern is responsible for the association. It is possible that sleep-wake disruption contributes to brain health changes. It is also possible that early or underlying health changes affect sleep, movement and daily rhythms before dementia is diagnosed. Observational research cannot fully separate these explanations.

## Does this mean sleeping longer prevents dementia?

No. The study does not show that simply increasing sleep duration prevents dementia. It examined combinations of measures rather than testing a sleep intervention.

It also does not establish that every person who naps, sleeps late or has occasional restless nights is at increased risk. The associations were found for specific combinations of measured characteristics in a research cohort.

Persistent sleep problems deserve attention because they can affect quality of life and may be linked to conditions that need assessment. Loud snoring, witnessed pauses in breathing, severe daytime sleepiness, persistent insomnia or major changes in sleep should be discussed with a healthcare professional.

## What can people in midlife and older age take from this?

The study is not a prescription, but it supports a broader way of thinking about sleep and healthy ageing.

It may be useful to pay attention to regularity, daylight exposure, daily movement and long periods of sitting, alongside sleep quality. A consistent routine and suitable physical activity are reasonable health habits for many adults, but the study did not test a specific programme or prove that these steps prevent dementia.

People with chronic pain, shift work, caregiving responsibilities, sleep disorders or other medical conditions may not be able to follow a fixed schedule. Advice should be practical and adapted to the person's circumstances.

## What are the limitations?

The cohort was made up of older adults from the UK, and the participants were healthier than the general population in some respects. The findings may not translate directly to every population, including people in India.

The study could not examine dementia subtypes. It also did not have data on sleep pathologies, and some profile groups had limited statistical power. Because the study is observational, it cannot show that changing a sleep-wake profile will reduce dementia risk.

The results are best understood as evidence of an association that deserves further investigation.

## The takeaway

This study adds to the growing evidence that brain health is connected to more than the number of hours spent in bed. Daily activity patterns, rest-activity rhythms and sleep characteristics appear to work together.

The practical lesson is not to chase a perfect sleep score or fear an occasional bad night. It is to consider sleep as one part of a wider pattern of healthy ageing, while seeking medical advice for persistent or disruptive sleep problems.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many people were included in the study?
The study included 3,973 dementia-free adults aged 60 and older, with a median follow-up of 12.3 years.

### Did poor sleep double the risk of dementia?
Two specific sleep-wake profiles were associated with hazard ratios close to 1.9 compared with the reference profile. This does not mean that poor sleep alone doubles an individual's risk.

### Does sleeping eight hours prevent dementia?
This study did not test an eight-hour sleep target or show that a specific duration prevents dementia.

### Why did researchers measure daytime activity?
The researchers examined the sleep-wake cycle as a combination of rest-activity rhythms, daytime movement, sleep and chronotype. Some risk patterns involved both low activity and disrupted sleep.

### Does the study apply directly to Indian adults?
Not necessarily. It used a UK cohort. Similar research in more diverse populations is needed before drawing population-specific conclusions.
