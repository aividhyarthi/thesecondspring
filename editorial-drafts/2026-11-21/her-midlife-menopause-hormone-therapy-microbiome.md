# Menopause Hormone Therapy and the Vaginal Microbiome: What a Study of 356 Women Found

Menopause hormone therapy is usually discussed in terms of symptoms such as hot flashes, night sweats and vaginal or urinary discomfort. Researchers are now examining another part of the picture: how hormone treatment may be associated with the communities of microbes living in different parts of the body.

A study published in *Nature Medicine* on 3 November 2026 followed 356 perimenopausal women who received oral estradiol and dydrogesterone for six months. The researchers reported changes in microbial communities, especially in the vaginocervical and urinary areas. They also found that the microbial profile present before treatment was associated with differences in response.

The findings add to research on the relationship between hormones and the microbiome. They do not mean that women need a microbiome test before considering hormone therapy, or that one microbial profile should determine treatment.

## What did the study examine?

The paper, *Heterogeneity of microbiome responses to menopausal hormone therapy among perimenopausal women*, examined 356 perimenopausal women who received oral estradiol and dydrogesterone over six months.

The researchers studied microbial communities at several body sites, including the gut, mouth, vaginocervical area and urinary tract. They also assessed clinical and symptom outcomes. The trial was registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry under ChiCTR2100049768.

The important point is that the research did not look at one single microbiome. Different body sites have different microbial communities and functions. A result from the gut cannot automatically be applied to the vagina or urinary tract.

## What changed after six months?

The researchers reported that the most pronounced microbial changes occurred in the vaginocervical and urinary niches. Changes in the oral microbiome were more moderate, while gut changes were minimal.

At the vaginocervical site, treatment was associated with an increase in *Lactobacillus crispatus* and a reduction in *Gardnerella vaginalis*. These organisms are often discussed in vaginal microbiome research, but the presence or absence of a single organism does not provide a complete diagnosis of vaginal or urinary health.

The paper also reported improvements in neuroendocrine, metabolic and symptom outcomes during the intervention. The study's analysis suggested that the microbial profile before treatment was associated with the degree of change observed.

## Why the starting microbiome appeared to matter

One of the study's more interesting findings concerned differences between women before treatment began.

Women with higher baseline levels of vaginocervical *Lactobacillus crispatus* showed greater subsequent colonisation at the vaginocervical and urinary sites and greater improvement in the Female Sexual Function Index. Women whose baseline communities were dominated by *Gardnerella vaginalis* showed a greater shift towards the microbial communities described by the researchers as healthier. Women with more diversified communities showed smaller improvements.

The researchers also reported differences in hormonal responsiveness according to baseline gut community type. These results raise the possibility that a person's microbial environment may be one factor associated with how she responds to treatment.

But this is not yet a clinical decision tool. The findings need replication, independent validation and evidence that microbiome-guided treatment improves outcomes compared with usual clinical assessment.

## What does this mean for vaginal and urinary health during perimenopause?

Hormonal changes around menopause can affect the tissues of the vagina and urinary tract. Some women experience vaginal dryness, discomfort during sex, irritation, urinary urgency or recurrent urinary symptoms. These symptoms can have more than one cause and should not automatically be attributed to menopause.

The microbiome is one area of active research. The study adds evidence that hormone exposure and microbial communities may be connected. It does not establish that every urinary symptom is caused by a microbiome imbalance, nor does it show that probiotics or commercial microbiome products can reproduce the study's findings.

Women with persistent urinary symptoms, pain, unusual discharge, bleeding or recurrent infections should seek appropriate clinical assessment. Symptoms that resemble a urinary infection may need testing and treatment rather than self-diagnosis.

## Does this change who should use menopause hormone therapy?

No. This study does not replace established clinical guidance for menopausal hormone therapy.

The decision to use hormone therapy depends on a woman's symptoms, medical history, age, time since menopause, individual risk factors, treatment goals and preferences. Different formulations and routes of administration may have different considerations. A qualified clinician should discuss benefits, risks and alternatives.

The research should not be interpreted as a recommendation for all perimenopausal women to take oral estradiol and dydrogesterone. Nor should it be used to encourage someone to stop an existing treatment.

## What remains uncertain?

Several questions remain before microbiome findings can influence routine care:

- Can the reported associations be reproduced in larger and more diverse populations?
- Do the microbial changes persist after treatment ends?
- Are the changes directly responsible for symptom improvement, or do they occur alongside other hormone-related effects?
- Can a reliable test identify who is likely to benefit from a particular treatment?
- Would microbiome-guided decisions improve outcomes compared with standard clinical care?

The study provides a reason to investigate these questions, not a ready-made answer.

## The takeaway

This six-month study of 356 perimenopausal women adds a new layer to research on menopause hormone therapy. It found site-specific changes in microbial communities, particularly in the vaginocervical and urinary areas, and reported that baseline microbiome patterns were associated with differences in response.

For now, the practical message is to understand the science without turning it into a commercial promise. Microbiome testing and supplements should not be treated as mandatory parts of menopause care on the basis of one study.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the vaginal microbiome?
It is the community of microorganisms living in the vaginal environment. Its composition varies between individuals and across life stages, including the menopause transition.

### Did this study include women taking hormone therapy?
Yes. It examined 356 perimenopausal women receiving oral estradiol and dydrogesterone for six months.

### Did hormone therapy change the gut microbiome?
The researchers reported minimal gut changes compared with more pronounced changes in the vaginocervical and urinary microbial communities.

### Does this mean women should get a vaginal microbiome test before hormone therapy?
No. The study does not establish microbiome testing as a standard way to select or monitor menopause treatment.

### Can probiotics replace menopause hormone therapy?
This study did not test probiotics as a replacement for hormone therapy. It provides no basis for substituting supplements for prescribed treatment.
